A beginner’s case-solving loop

You do not need to memorize every framework. Learn this six-step loop, then use the cards as specialized lenses. In an interview, explain your logic aloud and let evidence determine where you go deeper.

Step 1Clarify the decisionWhat must the client decide, by when, and how will success be measured?
Step 2Form a hypothesisState your early best guess. It guides priorities; it is allowed to change.
Step 3Build a structureSplit the problem into non-overlapping, testable branches tied to the objective.
Step 4Test the biggest branchAsk for data, calculate carefully, interpret the result, and update the hypothesis.
Step 5Create and compare optionsEvaluate value, feasibility, risk, timing, and reversibility—not just upside.
Step 6SynthesizeGive the answer first, then evidence, risks, mitigations, and immediate next steps.

Decision logic

Why this branch

A framework tells you what the boxes are. It does not tell you which box to open first, or what makes you switch. That judgement is what interviewers actually score, and it is the layer most notes skip. Every card below answers the same three questions: what decides the order, what each answer means, and what makes you change branch mid-case.

  1. Which branch can one question close?A branch you can rule out with a single data point comes first — not because it is more likely, but because it is cheaper. Price is one number. Volume is a hundred.
  2. Which branch did the prompt already point at?If the prompt says "revenue is flat but profits are down", the revenue branch is already closed. Start where the prompt left off.
  3. Where is the movement?Nothing broke in a branch that did not move. Ask for the trend on both sides before descending.
  4. What is the client's decision?In a decision case there is no cause to find. The order follows the gates: can we, should we, will it pay, how.
Scoping — 3CPORAlways, in the first 90 seconds, before any structure exists.
The structure
Objective · Company · Customer · Competition · Product · Context · Regulation
What decides the order

Objective first, always — and not as a formality. Everything downstream is judged against it. A 12% margin recovery in one year and a 12% margin recovery in five years are different cases with different answers. Then Company (what does it actually do and sell), then Context (how long, how much, where), and only then the rest as the case needs them.

Why that order, specifically

The prompt almost never states the objective. The casebook is explicit about this: it is the candidate's job to extract it. Candidates who skip it end up solving a problem the client did not have — and no amount of later rigour recovers that.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
The prompt names a number but no timelineAsk for the timeline before anything else. It decides whether cost levers (fast) or growth levers (slow) can even qualify.
The business is segmented at allAsk immediately whether the problem is concentrated in one segment or uniform. This single question collapses most profitability cases in two minutes.
You are tempted to ask about marginsStop. That is an analysis question, not a scoping one. Scoping questions shape your structure; analysis questions come after it.
The client is not a companyFind the real objective function. A government maximises welfare or votes, a hospital maximises outcomes per rupee. Profit trees are nonsense here.
What to ask, and what each answer means
What exactly are we trying to achieve, and by when?If they say 'restore profits to last year's level' you have a target. If they deflect — 'just diagnose it' — the case is a pure diagnosis and your synthesis should end at root cause plus options, not a financial plan.
Is the decline across the whole business, or concentrated?'Concentrated' hands you the answer's location for free. 'Uniform' is itself a finding — it points at something structural: an input cost, a regulation, an industry-wide price move.
Is this happening to competitors too?Yes means the cause is external and your recommendations must be about relative position, not internal fixes. No means look inward, and it is usually something the client changed.
How long has this been going on?A sharp break points at an event — a new entrant, a contract, a regulation. A slow drift points at structure — mix shift, ageing assets, gradual share loss.
Worked example
With numbers

Prompt: "Our client, a premium coffee chain, has seen same-store profits fall 15% in six months."

Four questions get you a long way. Objective: restore to prior level within a year. Segmentation: the drop is entirely in 8 of 40 outlets. Competitors: unaffected. Duration: started sharply four months ago.

You now know it is internal, recent, event-driven and geographically concentrated — before drawing a single box. The structure you draw next is about those 8 outlets, not about coffee.

Asking questions you will not use. Every scoping question should change a branch of the structure you are about to draw. If the answer would not change what you draw, it is curiosity, and curiosity costs you time you need later.
Profitability — the master treeProfits, margins or 'the bottom line' have fallen, stagnated, or lagged a benchmark.
The structure
Profit = Revenue − Cost
Revenue = Price × Volume
Cost  = Fixed + (Variable × Volume)
What decides the order

Neither branch first. Ask for both trends, then descend into the one that moved. This is the single most misunderstood point in case prep. There is no rule that says revenue comes before cost — there is a rule that says you do not descend blind.

Why that order, specifically

Opening a branch is expensive: it commits several minutes and signals a hypothesis. Asking 'what happened to revenue and what happened to cost over the same period' is one cheap question that eliminates half the tree. Candidates who default to revenue-first are following a habit, not a logic, and interviewers can tell.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Revenue fell, costs flatRevenue branch. Straightforward.
Revenue flat, costs roseCost branch. Straightforward.
Revenue grew, profit fellCost or mix — almost never pricing. Either cost grew faster than revenue, or the growth came in low-margin products. Check margin per unit before anything else.
Both moved in the hurting directionTake the larger one by absolute rupees, not by percentage. A 3% cost rise on a ₹900 cr base beats a 12% revenue fall on a ₹80 cr line.
Neither moved much, but profit fell sharplyYou are missing a segment or a one-off. Re-segment, and ask about exceptional items.
Volume grew and total cost grewSay out loud that absolute cost rising is expected. Compute cost per unit. If unit cost fell, the cost branch is closed and you have saved yourself ten minutes.
What to ask, and what each answer means
What happened to revenue and to cost over the same period?The branch-selecting question. Ask it before you draw anything below the first split. Both directions and both magnitudes, or the answer is useless.
Is it profit in absolute terms or margin percentage?Margin down with absolute profit up means growth at lower margin — a mix or pricing story. Absolute profit down means something is genuinely shrinking.
Which product or region drives most of the gap?Forces segmentation before diagnosis. Diagnosing a company-wide average is the most common way to spend twenty minutes and find nothing.
Is the cost rise in cost per unit or total cost?Separates a real efficiency problem from arithmetic. Total cost rising with volume is not a problem; cost per unit rising is.
Worked example
With numbers

Client's profit fell from ₹120 cr to ₹95 cr. You ask for both sides.

Revenue: ₹1,000 cr → ₹1,080 cr (+8%)
Cost: ₹880 cr → ₹985 cr (+12%)

Revenue grew. So the revenue branch is closed — and saying so out loud is worth marks. Costs grew faster, but volume also grew 8%, so check unit economics before concluding.

Cost per unit: 880/100 = ₹8.80 → 985/108 = ₹9.12 (+3.6%)

Unit cost genuinely rose. Now the value chain walk is justified, and you have a number to hunt: ₹0.32 per unit, ₹34.5 cr in total.

Descending before segmenting. A company-wide average hides the one region that is collapsing. Ask for the split by geography, product and channel before you open either branch — the answer often makes the branch choice obvious.
Revenue decomposition — price before volumeThe revenue branch is open: sales, volume, share, users or footfall have fallen.
The structure
Revenue = Price × Volume
Volume  = Customers × Ticket size × Frequency
Price   = f(list price, discount, mix)
What decides the order

Price first — because it is one question, and volume is twenty. This is your question, so here is the full answer.

Why that order, specifically

Three reasons, in order of weight. First, cost of elimination. Average realised price is a single number the client knows. One question either closes the branch or hands you the cause. Volume requires decomposing into customers, ticket size and frequency, each of which then splits into demand and supply drivers — that is a minimum of six questions to reach the same confidence. Cheap eliminations come first. Second, agency. Price is usually something the client decided — a discount scheme, a channel change, a new SKU mix. It is knowable instantly and fixable quickly. Volume is a market outcome: it is caused by customers and competitors, and diagnosing it means diagnosing them. Start with what the client controls. Third, and most important — price hides mix. If average realised price fell while list price never changed, you have not found a pricing problem; you have found a mix shift or a discounting problem, and those live in completely different parts of the tree. Candidates who go to volume first routinely miss this and spend the case hunting demand drivers for a problem that was on the price line all along.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Price fell, list price unchangedNot a pricing decision. It is mix (selling more cheap SKUs), discounting, or channel shift. Go there, not to demand.
Price fell and list price fellA deliberate decision. Ask why — a price war, an elasticity bet, a share grab. Then ask whether volume responded as intended. If it did not, that is the case.
Price flatClose the branch out loud and move to volume. You have just eliminated half of revenue for one question.
Price rose and revenue still fellVolume fell more than price rose — an elasticity story. Compute the implied elasticity; it is usually the crux.
Volume branch open, and it is B2CCustomers × ticket × frequency, then 4A (awareness, availability, accessibility, affordability) on whichever moved.
Volume branch open, and it is B2BCustomers × ticket × frequency still holds, but replace 4A with the sales funnel: leads, conversion, deal size, renewal.
What to ask, and what each answer means
What has average realised price done over the period?Realised, not list — the whole point. The gap between the two is discounting and mix, and that gap is where a surprising number of cases actually live.
Has list price changed?Paired with the previous answer this is the mix detector. Realised down, list flat means mix or discount, every time.
Is volume down in customers, in basket size, or in how often they buy?Three genuinely different problems. Fewer customers is acquisition or churn. Smaller baskets is assortment or affordability. Less frequency is engagement or substitution.
Is the volume drop on the demand side or the supply side?Stock-outs, capacity limits and distribution gaps look exactly like weak demand in the data and have opposite fixes. Ask early — it is the cheapest branch-closer in the volume tree.
Worked example
With numbers

Revenue fell 10%, from ₹500 cr to ₹450 cr. You ask about price first.

Average realised price: ₹200 → ₹180 (−10%)
Volume: 2.5 M units → 2.5 M units (flat)

Volume never moved. The entire fall is price. One question has closed the larger half of the tree. Now the follow-up that matters:

List price: ₹200 → ₹200 (unchanged)

So it is not a pricing decision at all. Realised price fell ₹20 with list price flat — that is ₹50 cr of discount or mix. You ask for the SKU split and find premium's share fell from 40% to 25%. This was never a pricing case. It is a premium demand collapse — and a candidate who opened the volume branch first would have seen flat volume, concluded revenue was fine, and missed it entirely.

Treating flat volume as 'no problem here'. Flat total volume can hide a violent mix shift underneath. Always ask for volume by segment before you close the volume branch, not just the total.
Cost side — the value chain walkThe cost branch is open, or the prompt names costs, margins or efficiency directly.
The structure
R&D → Raw material → Processing → Warehousing → Distribution → Marketing → After-sales
(then cross-cut: fixed vs variable, and per-unit vs absolute)
What decides the order

Walk the chain, not the P&L — and walk it in the direction the product moves. Then cross-cut fixed versus variable only after you have located the stage.

Why that order, specifically

The P&L tells you where cost landed in the accounts; the value chain tells you where it was created, which is the only place you can intervene. 'Logistics cost up 20%' is a P&L finding and it is useless — you cannot fix a line item. 'We moved to a fulfilment model that charges storage plus handling plus commission, while competitors use third-party logistics' is a value chain finding, and it tells you exactly what to change. Walking in product order also keeps you MECE without effort, because every cost attaches to a stage of the physical journey.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Total cost up, volume upCompute cost per unit before doing anything else. Half of these cases end here.
Fixed cost per unit roseNot a cost problem. It is a utilisation problem — the same fixed base spread over fewer units. The fix is on the volume side.
Variable cost per unit roseGenuine input or efficiency problem. Now walk the chain to find the stage.
One stage is out of line with competitorsThat is your answer. At every stage, ask how the client compares — a stage that is expensive in absolute terms but normal versus peers is not the story.
Costs rose with no operational changeLook outside: input prices, regulation, exchange rates, a supplier consolidating. Then the question becomes pass-through, not efficiency.
The chain looks fine everywhereYou are in the wrong branch, or the problem is mix. Go back and check margin by product.
What to ask, and what each answer means
Is the increase in cost per unit or total cost?Separates arithmetic from a real problem. Ask it first, every time.
Which stage of the chain has moved most?Forces the interviewer to hand you the location instead of you guessing stage by stage, which burns minutes.
How does that stage compare with competitors?The casebook flags this at every stage for a reason. Cost that is high but normal for the industry is not actionable; cost that is high relative to peers is.
Did we change anything upstream recently?Supplier, contract, plant, route, fulfilment model. A sharp cost break almost always has a decision behind it.
Worked example
With numbers

A ceiling fan manufacturer's costs are up, revenue stable. You ask for the split and learn the rise is specific to Amazon sales, not retail.

That kills most of the chain immediately — R&D, raw material and processing are shared across channels and cannot explain a channel-specific gap. You walk only the post-production stages and reach fulfilment.

The client uses Fulfilled-by-Amazon: storage, handling and commission. Competitors use cheaper third-party logistics with Amazon as a listing platform only. You ask for the per-unit cost of each head under both models.

The finding is not "logistics is expensive". It is a strategic choice of fulfilment model — which is fixable.

Cutting a cost that produces the revenue. Before recommending a cut, ask what that spend buys. Salesforce and marketing cuts look brilliant on the cost line and show up on the revenue line two quarters later.
Pricing — floor, reference, ceilingA number must be set or defended. Often a sub-case inside market entry rather than a case of its own.
The structure
Floor   = variable cost (+ allocated fixed, if you must recover it)
Ceiling = customer's willingness to pay
Anchor  = competitor / substitute price
Answer  = a point inside [floor, ceiling] with a stated reason
What decides the order

Establish the floor first, the ceiling second, the competitor anchor third. The floor because it is the only hard constraint — below it you lose money on every unit and nothing else matters. The ceiling because it sets how much room you are playing with. The anchor last because it only calibrates within a range you have already bounded.

Why that order, specifically

Candidates reach for competitor prices first because they are the easiest number to imagine. But a competitor price without a floor is dangerous — you can match a competitor and still lose money — and without a ceiling it is uninformative, because you do not know whether matching leaves value uncollected. Bound the range, then position inside it.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
New product, no comparable existsValue-based dominates. The ceiling is what the customer saves or gains by having it. Cost only sets the floor.
New product, comparables existCompetitor-based sets the anchor; value-based sets the deviation from it. Justify every rupee of premium or discount with a benefit.
Existing product, competitor movedDo not reflex-match. Check your cost position first — can you survive a price war? Then check whether their move is targeting your segment at all.
R&D is large and already spentConfirm whether it is sunk. If it is, it stays out of the unit price. Recovering sunk R&D through price is the most common casebook trap in pricing.
Channel sits between you and the customerWork in net realisation. Retail price minus distributor and retailer margin is what you actually earn — price the thing you earn, not the sticker.
You are asked for one numberGive a range with a recommended point and the reason. A single number with no range shows no reasoning.
What to ask, and what each answer means
What does the customer do today instead, and what does that cost them?The ceiling question. Willingness to pay is always relative to the alternative, including the alternative of doing nothing.
What is the variable cost per unit, and is the R&D sunk?The floor question, and the sunk-cost test in one.
What is the objective — share, margin, or cash?Decides where in the range you land. Penetration, skimming and margin maximisation are three different points and you must name which you are choosing.
How elastic is demand, and how will competitors respond?Stress-tests the number. A price that is right in a static world and wrong after retaliation is wrong.
Worked example
With numbers

A toothbrush that lasts a lifetime. Manufacturing cost ₹50. ₹10 M spent on R&D — confirmed sunk, so it stays out.

Floor: ₹50 plus a margin. Ceiling: what it replaces. A user buys 4 brushes a year at ₹40 for, say, 30 years.

Replacement value = 4 × ₹40 × 30 = ₹4,800, discounted for time and for the risk of loss or damage.

So the range is enormous — roughly ₹60 to ₹1,500 — and the real question is not the number but the model. At a one-off ₹800 you capture value once and never sell to that customer again. The interesting answer discusses subscription, warranty or replacement-guarantee models that keep a revenue line alive.

Cost-plus by reflex. It ignores what the customer will pay and is the most common pricing failure in the casebooks. Cost tells you when to walk away, never what to charge.
Market entryAnything the client does not do today — a geography, segment, category or channel.
The structure
Can we?  → company capability, resources, readiness
Should we? → market attractiveness, customer, competition
Will it pay? → size × share × price − cost, against a hurdle
How? → organic, JV, licence, acquisition + risk
What decides the order

Capability before attractiveness. Most candidates invert this and open with market size, because sizing feels like progress. It is the wrong order.

Why that order, specifically

Attractiveness is a property of the market; capability is a property of the client. An attractive market the client cannot serve is not an opportunity for them — it is an opportunity for someone else. Opening with capability also shapes everything after it: knowing the client has distribution muscle but no local brand tells you which entry mode to favour and which risks to stress, long before you get to the numbers. And it is the difference between a generic answer and a client-specific one, which is what the grade turns on.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Client has no transferable advantageThe answer is likely no, or acquire rather than build. Say so early and test it, rather than sizing a market you will then decline.
Market is attractive and client is capableNow size it — and size the <em>obtainable</em> share, not the total market.
Client wants speedAcquisition or JV. Greenfield is slow by definition; if time-to-market is the objective, greenfield is usually eliminated at the gate.
Client wants control or has proprietary technologyGreenfield or majority JV. Licensing gives away the thing that made entry viable.
Regulatory barrier existsTest it before economics. A regulation that caps foreign ownership or requires a local partner changes the mode question entirely and can kill the case.
The numbers work but only in the best caseSay so. A recommendation that survives only the optimistic scenario is a no with extra steps.
What to ask, and what each answer means
Why this market, and why now?Extracts the real objective — growth, defence against a rival, diversification, following a customer. Each implies a different bar for success.
What do we have that would make us win there?The capability gate. Brand, distribution, cost position, technology, relationships. 'Nothing specific' is a finding, and an important one.
What share could we realistically hold in three years, and who loses it?Forces a defensible share number. Share has to come from somewhere — naming the incumbent who loses it is what makes the estimate credible.
What does the client need to earn for this to clear their bar?Gives you a target to test against instead of producing a number with nothing to compare it to.
Worked example
With numbers

An EV manufacturer considering Southeast Asia. Resist sizing first.

Capability: strong battery technology, no local distribution, no service network, no brand recognition. That is one real advantage and three gaps — which already tells you the mode answer will lean towards partnership.

Only now size it, and size what you can hold:

Market 400 K units/yr, growing 25%
Realistic share by year 3: 5% = 20 K units
Price ₹1.2 L, contribution 20% = ₹24 K/unit
Contribution = 20,000 × ₹24,000 = ₹48 cr
Setup + network investment = ₹300 cr → payback beyond 6 years

Against a 4-year hurdle that fails. The recommendation becomes a JV with an existing distributor — which the capability analysis pointed at before a single number was calculated.

Presenting TAM as the opportunity. Size the total market, then the serviceable portion, then the share you can actually take, and defend that last number. Candidates who stop at TAM are quoting a statistic, not making a case.
GrowthThe business works and the client wants it bigger. Nothing is broken — that is what separates this from profitability.
The structure
Growth = Organic + Inorganic
Organic  = existing/new customers × existing/new products × existing/new geographies
Inorganic = acquire · partner · licence · acquire capability
What decides the order

Quantify the gap first, then size levers against it, and only then discuss them. The organic/inorganic split is the structure; the gap is what makes the structure useful.

Why that order, specifically

Growth cases fail on unsized lists. '3x in 3 years' is roughly 44% CAGR — an enormous number that immediately tells you incremental levers cannot get there alone, and that the answer must include something structural. Without computing that, every lever looks equally reasonable and you produce a brainstorm instead of a strategy. Sizing also does the prioritisation for you: a lever that cannot move a material share of the gap is a distraction, and saying so is a judgement the interviewer is watching for.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Target implies under ~15% CAGROrganic levers can plausibly carry it. Focus on penetration and share.
Target implies 15–30% CAGROrganic plus new products or geographies. Sequencing and capacity become the real questions.
Target implies above ~30% CAGRInorganic is almost certainly required. Say so early — it reframes the whole case.
Client has capital constraintsInorganic is limited. The answer shifts to partnerships, licensing and asset-light models.
Demand exists but delivery is constrainedThis is a capacity case wearing a growth costume. Salesforce, supply, talent. Growth cases fail here more often than on demand.
Growth would come at lower marginFlag it. 3x revenue at half the margin may not be the client's goal — check the objective before recommending it.
What to ask, and what each answer means
What is the current growth rate, and what is the target?Gives you the gap. Without both numbers you cannot prioritise anything.
Is the goal revenue, profit, or share?Three different strategies. Share can be bought with margin; profit cannot.
Where has past growth come from?Tells you which levers already work and are under-exploited, versus which have been tried and stalled.
What constrains us from growing faster today?Goes straight at the binding constraint. If the answer is capacity, the entire case is about capacity.
Worked example
With numbers

A fire extinguisher manufacturer wants 3x in 3 years, currently ₹100 cr growing 10%.

Target ₹300 cr in 3 years = 44% CAGR
Current trajectory: ₹133 cr → gap of ₹167 cr

Now size levers against ₹167 cr, not against each other:

Deeper penetration of existing accounts: ~₹30 cr
New geographies (South, East): ~₹45 cr
Adjacent products (alarms, suppression): ~₹40 cr
Total organic: ~₹115 cr — short by ₹52 cr

The gap cannot be closed organically. That conclusion — reached by arithmetic rather than assertion — is the case. The answer is organic levers plus an acquisition of a regional player, and the ₹52 cr sizes the acquisition.

Listing levers without sizing them. An unsized list is brainstorming. The moment you attach a number to each lever, the priority order and the need for inorganic growth both fall out on their own.
M&A and due diligenceOwnership changes hands — acquire, merge, divest, invest, exit.
The structure
Rationale → Target fit → Synergies (revenue + cost) → Valuation → Integration & risk
What decides the order

Rationale before target, always. And within synergies, cost before revenue.

Why that order, specifically

Two sequencing points. First, rationale before target: if the client cannot say why acquisition beats building or partnering, no target analysis matters — you would be optimising the wrong decision. Second, cost synergies before revenue synergies, because cost synergies are far more certain. Removing a duplicate warehouse is arithmetic; cross-selling into the acquired customer base is a hypothesis about behaviour. Deals justified mainly on revenue synergies are the ones that destroy value, and leading with them signals you have not seen that pattern.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Question is 'should we acquire'Market entry logic plus strategic fit. Build-buy-partner is the real comparison.
Question is 'which target'3CP on each target, plus fit against the stated rationale. Score, do not narrate.
Question is 'how much to pay'Standalone value plus the share of synergies you are willing to pay away. Name the walk-away price — it is the discipline the question is testing.
Synergies are mostly revenueDiscount them hard and say why. Also ask what has to be true about customer behaviour for them to land.
Cultures or systems differ sharplyIntegration risk dominates. This is where stated deal value is usually lost, and naming it is worth marks.
Financial buyer rather than strategicThe order inverts: fund mandate, hold period and portfolio conflicts come before target quality, and the exit route is half the thesis.
What to ask, and what each answer means
Why acquire rather than build this ourselves?The rationale gate. If building is cheaper and only slower, the case is about time-to-market, not about the target.
What exactly are we buying — customers, technology, a team, or capacity?Determines which risks matter. Buying a team means retention risk dominates; buying capacity means utilisation does.
Which synergies are cost and which are revenue, and over what period?Forces the split and the timeline. Synergies realised in year four are worth a fraction of synergies in year one.
What is the walk-away price?The question most candidates never ask, and the one that shows commercial judgement.
Worked example
With numbers

A consumer goods company evaluating a niche organic skincare brand.

Rationale first: they want entry into premium natural care, where they have no credible brand and where building one takes five to seven years. Acquisition is justified on time, not on cost — and that framing sets the valuation bar.

Target: ₹200 cr revenue, 18% EBITDA = ₹36 cr
Cost synergies: procurement + distribution overlap ≈ ₹12 cr, realisable in 18 months
Revenue synergies: shelf access in 40,000 outlets — claimed ₹25 cr, discounted to ₹10 cr for channel-fit risk
Combined EBITDA ≈ ₹58 cr

At a 12x multiple the ceiling is around ₹700 cr. Paying more means paying away all the synergy — and that number is the walk-away price.

Counting synergies gross. Net off the cost to achieve them and the time to realise them. A deal that works on gross synergies and fails on net is the standard way real acquisitions destroy value.
Guesstimates and market sizingA number has to be built from nothing — standalone, or inside a market entry at the sizing step.
The structure
Top-down:  universe → filters → answer
Bottom-up: unit → scale → answer
Bottleneck: binding constraint → throughput → answer
What decides the order

Choose the approach by where the reliable number is, and say which you are using before you start.

Why that order, specifically

The three approaches are not interchangeable and the choice is itself graded. Top-down works when you trust a big aggregate — population, households, GDP — and can apply defensible filters. Bottom-up works when you trust a small unit — revenue per store, output per machine — and can count the units. Bottleneck works when something physical caps the answer regardless of demand: runways, seats, shifts, GPU hours. Picking the one with the most reliable anchor is what separates a defensible estimate from an invented one, and announcing the choice shows the interviewer you made it deliberately.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Durable good (fridge, car, chimney)Households, not population. Then penetration, then units per household, then replacement cycle. Missing the replacement layer is the standard error.
Consumable (chocolate, shampoo)Population, then consumption frequency. Segment by usage intensity, not just demographics.
Anything with a physical capBottleneck. Runways for flights, seats × turns for restaurant covers, capacity for units produced.
B2B marketCount firms, not people. Firms × spend per firm, usually segmented by size band.
The answer feels implausibleSay so and fix an assumption out loud. Catching your own error is worth more than the number was.
You have five or more segmentsCollapse them. Three or four clean segments you can carry through the arithmetic beat eight you cannot.
What to ask, and what each answer means
Units per year or rupees per year?Scope, and it changes the whole calculation. Ask before starting.
New sales only, or including replacement?For durables this frequently doubles the answer. It is the most commonly missed clarification in sizing.
Which geography and which segment?Prevents sizing something far larger than the case needs.
Should I go top-down or bottom-up here?Asking shows you know they differ. Many interviewers will tell you, which saves you from the harder path.
Worked example
With numbers

Size the annual market for electric kitchen chimneys in India.

Population 1.4 bn ÷ 4.4 per household ≈ 320 M households
Urban 35% ≈ 112 M
Target: upper and middle income ≈ 30% ≈ 34 M households
Penetration of modular kitchens ≈ 25% ≈ 8.5 M relevant households
Chimneys per household ≈ 1.15 (some larger homes have two)
Installed base ≈ 9.8 M
Replacement cycle 10 yrs → replacement demand ≈ 1 M/yr
New installations ≈ 0.6 M/yr
Total ≈ 1.6 M units × ₹15,000 ≈ ₹2,400 cr

Then sanity-check: 1.6 M units against 34 M target households implies about 5% of them buy each year. For a 10-year durable at 25% penetration, plausible.

Delivering the number without a check. 'That implies four per household, which is too high — let me revisit frequency' earns more than a clean number does. Interviewers are testing whether you can catch yourself.
Abstract and custom structuresNo standard type fits, the client is not profit-maximising, or the goal sounds soft.
The structure
Stakeholder cut: each party → objective → constraint → lever
Process cut:     stage 1 → stage 2 → … → diagnose each
Metric cut:      goal → equation that produces it → attack each term
What decides the order

Pick the cut from the shape of the problem, not from habit. Stakeholder when several parties have conflicting objectives. Process when something moves through stages. Metric when a soft-sounding goal hides a quantity.

Why that order, specifically

The casebook is explicit that these cases exist to punish memorised frameworks — increasingly so at McKinsey and BCG. The skill being tested is building structure from the problem rather than retrieving one. The metric cut is the most underused and often the most powerful, because goals that sound unquantifiable usually are not: footfall, rankings and satisfaction all decompose into equations you can attack term by term.

Decision rules
If you seeThen
Several parties with conflicting aimsStakeholder cut. Government, citizens, operators, staff — each with an objective and a lever.
Something moves through stagesProcess cut. Application, approval, disbursement. Or awareness, visit, purchase, return.
The goal is a soft-sounding metricMetric cut. Write the equation that produces it, then attack each term.
The client is not a companyFind the real objective function before structuring. Welfare, outcomes per rupee, votes, attendance.
It is a personal or judgement situationImmediate action, root cause, prevention. Treat it as a mini-consulting problem with a stakeholder cut.
You genuinely cannot see a cutFall back to internal versus external, or supply versus demand. Both are always MECE and always defensible.
What to ask, and what each answer means
What does success look like here, and for whom?Extracts the objective function when there is no profit line. Different stakeholders will have different answers, and that conflict is often the case.
Who are all the parties affected?Builds the stakeholder cut and usually surfaces one the prompt never mentioned.
Is there a number underneath this goal?Turns a soft goal into a decomposable one. Almost always yes.
What is in the client's control?Separates levers from context. A brilliant analysis of something the client cannot change is worth nothing.
Worked example
With numbers

A temple town wants more tourists.

Not a profit tree — the client is a municipal body and the objective may be economic activity, not revenue. Use the metric cut:

Tourists = Awareness × Intent × Access × Conversion × Repeat

Each term is now attackable and measurable. Awareness: marketing reach, travel platforms, religious circuits. Intent: what competing destinations offer. Access: flight and rail connectivity, road quality, last mile. Conversion: accommodation capacity and quality, safety. Repeat: experience quality, festival calendar.

The follow-up that shows judgement: ask whether the objective is tourist numbers or tourist spend. They lead to opposite recommendations — volume means budget access, spend means premium experience.

Forcing profitability onto a non-profit client. A municipality's objective function is not profit. Find out what it actually is before you structure, or every branch below will be answering the wrong question.
Component detail

The lenses, part by part

These are analytical tools you use inside a branch, not case structures. Each card breaks the framework into its parts and says what every part means, what makes it strong or weak, and what it does to the answer.

Porter's Five ForcesJudging whether an industry is worth being in — market entry, M&A, or a 'should we exit' prompt.
What it is for

Five forces decide how much of the value an industry creates is kept by the firms in it, rather than competed away to customers or absorbed by suppliers. It answers is this industry structurally profitable — not whether your client will win in it. That is a different question, and confusing the two is the classic misuse.

Each part, and what it actually does
Threat of new entrants
Means
How easily someone new can start competing with you.
Driven by
Economies of scale · capital required · brand strength and switching costs · access to distribution · proprietary technology or patents · regulatory licences · expected retaliation from incumbents · time-compression diseconomies (things money cannot buy quickly, like a service network).
So what
It caps your price even when nobody actually enters. If you could raise price 20% and stay profitable, but doing so would invite entry, the threat alone has set your ceiling. This is why high-margin industries with low barriers do not stay high-margin.
Bargaining power of buyers
Means
Your customers' ability to force your price down or demand more for the same price.
Driven by
Buyer concentration (few large buyers) · the share of your sales they represent · how undifferentiated your product is · their switching cost · price transparency · their own margin pressure (squeezed buyers squeeze you) · a credible threat to make it themselves.
So what
It transfers value from you to them. In practice it shows up as discounting, long payment terms, tender processes and annual price-down clauses. A modern retailer negotiating with an FMCG supplier is the standard example.
Bargaining power of suppliers
Means
Your input providers' ability to raise your costs or degrade your terms.
Driven by
Few credible suppliers · differentiated input with no substitute · high switching cost · the input is critical to your product's quality · you are a small customer to them · they could forward-integrate into your business.
So what
It compresses your margin from below, exactly as buyer power compresses it from above. Between the two, margin is whatever is left. Use the heuristic when you get confused: you pay a supplier, a buyer pays you.
Threat of substitutes
Means
A different way of meeting the same customer need — not a rival product, a rival solution.
Driven by
The price-performance of the substitute · the customer's switching cost · their propensity to substitute at all · whether the substitute is improving faster than you are.
So what
It caps the industry's ceiling price. Video calls substitute for business flights; cement substitutes for steel in some construction. The substitutes that matter most are the ones produced by industries earning better returns than yours, because they have room to attack on price.
Rivalry among existing competitors
Means
How hard the firms already in the industry fight each other.
Driven by
Number and balance of competitors (many equal-sized is worse than one dominant) · industry growth rate (slow growth forces share-stealing) · fixed cost intensity (high fixed costs push everyone to fill capacity at any price) · lack of differentiation · high exit barriers (firms that cannot leave keep competing at a loss) · capacity added in big lumps.
So what
It determines whether competition happens on price or on value. Airlines are the textbook worst case: high fixed costs, low differentiation, slow growth, brutal exit barriers — which is why the industry creates enormous value and keeps almost none of it.
Using it without reciting it
  • Name the two that bind, not all five. Reciting five forces at equal depth is the single most common way to waste four minutes. Say which two actually determine profitability here and why, then mention the others in one line.
  • Rate each one and say so. 'Supplier power is high because GPU supply is concentrated in one vendor' is analysis. 'Supplier power is a consideration' is filler.
  • Follow it with a conclusion. The forces are an input, not an answer. End with what they mean for the decision on the table — enter, don't enter, enter only in this segment.
What it does not do. It describes an industry, not a company — it cannot tell you whether your client specifically will win, which is what VRIO or a capability assessment is for. It is a snapshot, so in fast-moving industries it ages quickly. And it has no force for complements or for regulation as an actor rather than a barrier, so in platform and regulated businesses you have to add those yourself.
Worked example
Applied

Application-layer AI products, assessed honestly:

Entrants: very high. Open-source models and cloud infrastructure have collapsed the capital requirement. Suppliers: high and concentrated. GPU supply and foundation-model providers can change terms unilaterally. Buyers: high. Many near-identical vendors and minimal switching cost between wrappers. Substitutes: moderate. Manual work and legacy tools still serve many buyers. Rivalry: intense. Fast innovation, talent war, overlapping products.

Four of five are unfavourable, which is the finding: defensibility cannot come from the model. It has to come from proprietary data, workflow embedding or distribution. That conclusion is what the framework was for — and it is the insight interviewers listen for in any AI entry or investment case.

Porter's Value ChainCost problems, operational bottlenecks, or 'where in this business does value actually get created'.
What it is for

Splits a firm into the activities that create value, so you can ask where cost accumulates and where advantage comes from. In cases it is mostly a cost-location tool: the P&L tells you where cost landed in the accounts, the value chain tells you where it was created — which is the only place you can act.

Each part, and what it actually does
Primary activities
Means
The sequence the product physically moves through.
Driven by
Inbound logistics (raw material, supplier terms, wastage) · Operations (machinery, labour, utilisation, defects, packaging) · Outbound logistics (warehousing, inventory holding, transport) · Marketing and sales (channels, ad spend per channel, salesforce, promotions) · Service (repairs, spares, returns, CRM).
So what
Walking these in order keeps you MECE without effort, because every cost attaches to a stage of the journey. It also makes the interviewer's job easy, which matters more than candidates think.
Support activities
Means
Things that serve every stage rather than sitting in the sequence.
Driven by
Firm infrastructure (finance, legal, planning) · HR · Technology development and R&D · Procurement as a function.
So what
Usually where overhead hides. If primary activities all look normal and costs are still high, this is where to look — and it is the branch most candidates forget exists.
The comparison at every stage
Means
How the client's cost at each stage compares with competitors.
Driven by
Same activity, peer benchmark, per unit.
So what
This is the step that converts a list into a finding. A stage that is expensive in absolute terms but normal for the industry is not your answer; a stage that is out of line with peers is. The ISB casebook flags this at every single stage, and it is the difference between describing a cost base and diagnosing one.
Using it without reciting it
  • Walk it in product order. Do not jump around — the sequence is the MECE guarantee.
  • Ask which stage moved before walking all of them. One question can skip six.
  • Always separate per-unit from absolute. Volume growth raises total cost at every stage; that is not a problem.
What it does not do. It is built for manufacturing and does not map cleanly onto software or services — for those, substitute the delivery chain (acquisition → onboarding → usage → support → renewal) or the cost-to-serve model. It also says nothing about whether the cost is worth incurring; that is a margin question, not a chain question.
Worked example
Applied

Costs up, revenue stable. You ask which stage moved and learn the rise is specific to online sales, not retail.

That single answer eliminates R&D, raw material and operations immediately — those are shared across channels and cannot produce a channel-specific gap. You walk only outbound logistics, marketing and service, and reach fulfilment: the client uses a model that charges storage plus handling plus commission, where competitors use cheaper third-party logistics.

A P&L reading would have said 'logistics cost up 20%'. The chain reading says which model choice caused it, which is the thing you can change.

BCG Growth–Share MatrixA portfolio decision across multiple products, brands or business units. Never with one product.
What it is for

Plots each business unit on market growth (vertical) against relative market share (horizontal). The logic underneath is cash: growth consumes cash, share generates it. The matrix is really a cash-allocation map wearing a 2×2.

Each part, and what it actually does
Star — high growth, high share
Means
Leading in a fast-growing market.
Driven by
Generates a lot of cash but consumes just as much defending position in a growing market.
So what
Invest. Today's stars become tomorrow's cash cows when the market matures — that transition is the whole point of holding them.
Cash cow — low growth, high share
Means
Leading in a mature market.
Driven by
Generates far more cash than it needs, because a slow market requires little investment to hold share.
So what
Milk it, and fund the stars and selected question marks with the proceeds. Over-investing here is a classic error — you cannot buy growth in a market that has none.
Question mark — high growth, low share
Means
Present in an attractive market but not winning it.
Driven by
Consumes cash heavily and returns little, because holding position in a growing market is expensive when you lack scale.
So what
The only quadrant demanding an actual decision: invest enough to make it a star, or exit. Funding it at a level that sustains without winning is the worst option and the most common one.
Dog — low growth, low share
Means
Weak position in a market going nowhere.
Driven by
Roughly cash-neutral, often quietly consuming management attention.
So what
Divest, reposition, or harvest. The nuance worth voicing: a dog that shares fixed costs or completes a product line may be worth keeping for reasons the matrix cannot see.
Using it without reciting it
  • Use relative share, not absolute. Share versus the largest competitor is the measure — 20% share is strong against a 10% leader and weak against a 50% one.
  • Size the bubbles. Saying which units are large by revenue turns a 2×2 into a prioritisation.
  • State the cash logic out loud. That is the insight; the quadrant names are just labels.
What it does not do. It assumes share drives profitability, which fails in niche and premium businesses where small share and high margin coexist. It ignores synergies between units entirely. And 'market' is defined by you — define it narrowly and everything becomes a star.
Worked example
Applied

A diversified FMCG player with four lines: soaps (low growth, leading share), organic personal care (high growth, small share), biscuits (low growth, small share), premium haircare (high growth, leading share).

Soaps are the cash cow and fund everything. Premium haircare is the star and takes the investment. Organic personal care is the question mark and needs the decision — the answer usually being acquire a brand rather than build share slowly. Biscuits is the dog, and the interesting question is whether it shares distribution with soaps, because that changes the divest answer.

Ansoff MatrixChoosing a growth direction, or structuring the organic half of a growth case.
What it is for

Four growth directions, ordered by how much new ground you are breaking — and therefore by risk. Existing products and existing markets is the safest; new products in new markets is the riskiest. Its real use in a case is as a risk-ordering device, not a list.

Each part, and what it actually does
Market penetration — existing product, existing market
Means
Sell more of what you have to the people you already serve.
Driven by
Increase usage frequency, take share from rivals, convert non-users in the same segment, improve distribution density.
So what
Cheapest and least risky, because you are not learning anything new. Should almost always be exhausted before the others — and interviewers notice when candidates skip straight to diversification.
Product development — new product, existing market
Means
Sell something new to customers you already understand.
Driven by
Line extensions, adjacent categories, bundles, services attached to a product.
So what
Moderate risk. You know the customer, which is the hard part; you are betting on your ability to build. Leans on R&D and speed.
Market development — existing product, new market
Means
Take what you have to people you do not serve yet.
Driven by
New geographies, new segments, new channels, new use cases for the same product.
So what
Moderate risk from the other direction. You know the product works; you are betting you can reach and convert an unfamiliar customer. Leans on distribution and local knowledge.
Diversification — new product, new market
Means
Both new at once.
Driven by
Related (shares technology or channel with the core) or unrelated (pure portfolio play).
So what
Highest risk by a wide margin, because both unknowns compound. Usually justified only by acquisition rather than build, or by a structural threat to the core business.
Using it without reciting it
  • Order your recommendations by the matrix. Penetration first, diversification last, and say why — that ordering is the judgement.
  • Size each quadrant against the growth gap. Otherwise you have four labels, not a strategy.
  • Name what has to be built for each. Market development needs distribution; product development needs R&D. The capability requirement often decides the answer.
What it does not do. It says nothing about how attractive each direction is — only how risky. Pair it with market attractiveness, or you will recommend the safest option into a dying market.
Worked example
Applied

A regional restaurant chain wanting to double revenue in three years.

Penetration: more visits per customer via loyalty and daypart expansion — cheap, maybe 20% of the gap. Product development: packaged sauces and meal kits to existing customers — moderate, 15%. Market development: new cities with the same format — moderate, 40%. Diversification: cloud kitchens under new brands — risky, the remainder.

The sizing shows penetration alone cannot get there, which justifies taking on market development risk. That reasoning, not the 2×2, is the answer.

4Ps and 7Ps — the marketing mixDiagnosing why a product is underperforming commercially, or designing a launch.
What it is for

The levers a firm controls in taking something to market. In a diagnostic case it is a checklist for finding which lever broke; in a launch case it is the design specification. The last three Ps exist because services cannot be separated from how they are delivered.

Each part, and what it actually does
Product
Means
What is actually being sold, and its attributes.
Driven by
Features, quality, range and depth, packaging, branding, warranty, the value proposition versus substitutes.
So what
Ask what changed. A reformulation, a pack-size change or an SKU cut is behind a surprising share of demand problems.
Price
Means
What the customer pays, and the structure of it.
Driven by
List price, realised price, discounts, elasticity, price relative to competitors, payment terms.
So what
In diagnosis, always separate list from realised — the gap is discounting and mix, and it is where cases hide.
Place
Means
How it reaches the customer.
Driven by
Channels, distribution reach, retailer margins, stock availability, online versus offline mix, supply chain.
So what
The most under-examined P in Indian cases and often the answer. A product nobody can find looks exactly like a product nobody wants.
Promotion
Means
How the customer learns about it and is persuaded.
Driven by
Advertising spend and mix, message, salesforce, trade promotions, digital and word of mouth, brand perception.
So what
Check effectiveness, not just spend. Promotions that move volume while destroying margin are a common finding.
People (services)
Means
Who delivers the service and how well.
Driven by
Training, attitude, expertise, staffing levels, incentives.
So what
In service businesses this often is the product. Poor service shows up as churn long before it shows up in a complaint log.
Process (services)
Means
The steps the customer goes through to receive it.
Driven by
Waiting time, handovers, turnaround, quality assurance, failure recovery.
So what
Where service cost and service quality are jointly determined. Lengthening a process to cut cost usually costs more in churn than it saves.
Physical evidence (services)
Means
The tangible cues around an intangible service.
Driven by
Premises, ambience, packaging, documentation, digital interface, uniforms.
So what
How customers judge quality when they cannot judge the service itself. It disproportionately drives perceived value and therefore willingness to pay.
Using it without reciting it
  • Do not walk all seven. Use the mix to generate hypotheses, then test the two or three the evidence points at.
  • In diagnosis, ask what changed in each P recently. That converts a static checklist into a timeline, which is far more useful.
  • For services, lead with People, Process and Physical evidence — the first four are usually not where service businesses break.
What it does not do. It is entirely internal and firm-controlled — no competitors, no macro, no regulation. Pair it with 3C or PESTEL if the cause might be outside the firm.
Worked example
Applied

A packaged snack brand's volumes are falling. Walking the mix with 'what changed' attached:

Product — pack size cut from 80g to 70g at the same price nine months ago. Price — list unchanged, so realised price per gram rose 14%. Place — unchanged. Promotion — unchanged.

One question per P located the cause in two minutes. It was a disguised price increase, and the volume response is an elasticity story — which is where the rest of the case goes.

The 4AsDemand-side diagnosis, especially consumer and rural India cases. Sits under the volume branch.
What it is for

Four conditions that must all hold before a purchase happens. Because they are sequential gates rather than parallel factors, a failure at any one makes the others irrelevant — which is precisely what makes it a good diagnostic: you can test them in order and stop at the first that fails.

Each part, and what it actually does
Awareness
Means
Does the customer know the product exists and what it does?
Driven by
Advertising reach and frequency, channel presence, word of mouth, brand recall, clarity of message.
So what
Test first. If people do not know, nothing downstream matters, and every other fix is wasted spend.
Availability
Means
Can they find it when they want it?
Driven by
Distribution reach, stock-outs, replenishment cycles, shelf space, retailer willingness to stock, opening hours.
So what
The gap between a brand's ambition and its distribution is a recurring Indian case finding. Ask for numeric distribution and weighted distribution separately if you can.
Accessibility
Means
Can they physically or practically get to it?
Driven by
Distance, last-mile, digital access and literacy, delivery, format suitability for their context.
So what
Distinct from availability: the product may be in a store the customer cannot reasonably reach. In rural cases this is often the binding gate.
Affordability
Means
Can they pay for it in the form it is offered?
Driven by
Absolute price versus income, pack size and unit price, credit and instalments, total cost of ownership.
So what
Affordability is about the ticket, not the price. Smaller packs and EMI have solved far more Indian affordability problems than price cuts have.
Using it without reciting it
  • Test them in order and stop at the first failure. That is the efficiency the framework buys you.
  • Attach a number to each gate where you can. 'Aware 60%, available in 30% of outlets, of whom we convert half' turns the framework into a funnel you can size.
  • Distinguish availability from accessibility explicitly. Candidates collapse them and lose the distinction that usually matters.
What it does not do. It is purely demand-side — it will not find a supply constraint, a cost problem or a competitive move. And it assumes the customer wants the product at all; desire and need sit upstream of all four gates.
Worked example
Applied

A hygiene brand's rural volumes are flat despite heavy advertising.

Awareness: 70% aided recall — the gate is passing, so the advertising is not the problem. Availability: present in 22% of rural outlets against a competitor's 65% — the gate fails here. Accessibility and affordability never get tested, because the answer is already located.

More advertising against 22% distribution would have burned budget against a wall. The 4As found that in two questions.

PESTELExternal risk assessment — usually near the end of a market entry or M&A recommendation, not the start.
What it is for

Six categories of forces outside the firm's control. Its honest role in a case is risk enumeration at the end, not diagnosis at the beginning. Used as an opening structure it produces a long list of things that might matter, which is the opposite of analysis.

Each part, and what it actually does
Political
Means
Government action and stability.
Driven by
Policy direction, trade and tariff regimes, subsidies, political stability, state-level variation, elections.
So what
Matters most in regulated, licensed or subsidised sectors — energy, telecom, defence, agriculture.
Economic
Means
Macro conditions that shape demand and cost.
Driven by
Growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, income distribution, employment.
So what
Drives both willingness to pay and cost of capital. In entry cases it is usually the currency and interest rate that bite, not GDP growth.
Social
Means
Demographics, culture and behaviour.
Driven by
Age structure, urbanisation, household size, tastes, health and sustainability attitudes, family and gender norms.
So what
Slow-moving, so it explains trends rather than shocks. If the prompt describes a gradual drift, look here.
Technological
Means
Capability shifts that change what is possible or cheap.
Driven by
Automation, digitisation, platform shifts, R&D intensity, adoption rates.
So what
Cuts both ways — it creates the substitute that attacks you and the tool that defends you. Name which direction it runs for this client.
Environmental
Means
Physical and ecological factors.
Driven by
Climate and seasonality, resource scarcity, emissions, waste, supply chain exposure.
So what
Increasingly a cost and compliance driver rather than a reputational one. Seasonality is the version that shows up most in Indian cases.
Legal
Means
Binding rules and liabilities.
Driven by
Licensing, competition law, labour law, data protection, product standards, contract enforceability.
So what
Often the hard gate in entry cases — an ownership cap or licensing requirement can end the case, so test it before the economics.
Using it without reciting it
  • Name two or three, not six. Only the forces that materially change the decision earn airtime.
  • Attach a mitigation to each risk you raise. A risk without a mitigation is a worry; with one it is advice.
  • Put it at the end. Opening with PESTEL signals you have no hypothesis.
What it does not do. It lists forces without weighting them, says nothing about competitors or customers, and has no mechanism for how a force translates into the client's P&L. That translation is your job, and it is what distinguishes a good PESTEL from a recital.
Worked example
Applied

A European EV maker entering Southeast Asia. Only three of the six move the decision:

Political — local content requirements and import duty determine whether assembly must be local, which changes the capital requirement by an order of magnitude. Economic — currency volatility against a euro cost base compresses margin unpredictably; hedge or price in local currency. Technological — charging infrastructure density caps addressable demand regardless of product quality.

Social, environmental and legal get one line together. That is a PESTEL doing work.

3C and 3CPScoping almost any case, and structuring a business-situation assessment.
What it is for

The three parties whose interaction determines whether a business works, plus the product as a fourth when the offer itself is in question. Its virtue is speed — it is short enough to run from memory under pressure, which is exactly what you need in the first ninety seconds.

Each part, and what it actually does
Company
Means
What the client is and does.
Driven by
Revenue streams, geographies, position in the value chain, scale, capabilities, cost structure, goals and constraints.
So what
Establishes what is actually on the table. Also surfaces the segmentation you will need almost immediately.
Customer
Means
Who buys and why.
Driven by
Segments, needs, purchase behaviour, willingness to pay, B2B versus B2C mix, who decides versus who uses.
So what
In B2B, separating the buyer from the user is often the whole insight — they have different criteria and the product serves one while being sold to the other.
Competition
Means
Who else is serving that need.
Driven by
Direct rivals, substitutes, market shares, recent moves, cost positions, whether the problem is industry-wide.
So what
The 'is this happening to competitors too' question lives here, and it is one of the highest-yield questions in any case.
Product (the P)
Means
The offer itself.
Driven by
SKU mix, USP, lifecycle stage, complements and substitutes, patents, premium-ness.
So what
Add it when the case is about what is being sold rather than how the business is run — pricing, launch, portfolio.
Using it without reciting it
  • Use it to scope, then drop it. 3C is a question generator, not a case structure. Do not present it as your framework.
  • For every segmentation you learn, ask whether the problem is concentrated or uniform. This is the highest-value habit in scoping.
  • Extend to 3CPOR for a fuller scope — adding Objectives, Context and Regulation, which is how the ISB book runs it.
What it does not do. No internal operations, no cost structure detail, no macro. It tells you the players, not the mechanism.
Worked example
Applied

Prompt: a credit card issuer's profits are declining.

Company — issuer, not a network; revenue from interchange, interest and annual fees; India only. Customer — split by salaried and self-employed, revolvers and transactors. Competition — industry-wide margin pressure from an interchange cap.

Three questions and the case has already narrowed: an external regulatory change affecting one revenue stream, with the segment split telling you where the remaining levers are.

Customer journey / AIDADemand-side problems where decomposition has stalled — falling conversion, footfall or engagement.
What it is for

Traces the path from not knowing a product exists to buying it again. Its power is that it is a funnel, so it is quantifiable: put a number on each stage and the drop-off locates itself. AIDA is the compact version; the pre/during/post split is the fuller one.

Each part, and what it actually does
Pre-purchase — need and awareness
Means
Does the customer have the need, and do they know you meet it?
Driven by
Need triggers, promotions, brand recall, word of mouth, search behaviour.
So what
A weak top of funnel makes everything below it look broken when it is not.
Pre-purchase — accessibility and affordability
Means
Can they reach you and pay you?
Driven by
Distribution, location, delivery, price versus income, credit and ticket size.
So what
The 4As live here; use them as the sub-structure for this stage.
During purchase — availability and experience
Means
Is it there, and is buying it pleasant?
Driven by
Stock-outs, assortment, ambience, staff, queue time, payment friction, the six senses in retail.
So what
In physical retail this is where conversion is won and lost, and it is invisible in sales data.
During purchase — selection and action
Means
Do they choose you over the alternative in front of them?
Driven by
Comparison with substitutes, complements and bundles, shelf position, final packaging, billing.
So what
Everything up to here got them ready to buy something. This stage decides whether it is yours.
Post-purchase — service and repeat
Means
Do they come back?
Driven by
After-sales, returns, repairs, spares, CRM, loyalty, the gap between expectation and delivery.
So what
Where lifetime value is made. A retention problem read as an acquisition problem is one of the most expensive misdiagnoses in these cases.
Using it without reciting it
  • Attach conversion rates and multiply. A funnel without numbers is a list; with numbers it points at the stage.
  • Use it as the fallback when standard decomposition dries up. That is what the casebook recommends it for.
  • Split new from repeat customers. The two journeys are different and mixing them hides retention problems.
What it does not do. Nothing about cost, supply or competitors' actions. It also assumes a linear path, which fits considered purchases better than impulse ones.
Worked example
Applied

A retailer's revenue is falling with footfall stable. Numbers by stage:

Footfall 100 → browse 100 → try 40 → purchase 12 → return within 3 months 3

Versus last year, try-to-purchase fell from 45% to 30% while everything upstream held. The problem is at the moment of decision — pricing at the shelf, assortment depth, or a competitor's offer — not awareness or traffic. That is a completely different set of fixes from the one a footfall story would have produced.

Breakeven, contribution and elasticityAny case with a pricing, volume or investment decision — which is most of them.
What it is for

Three small pieces of arithmetic that decide more cases than any 2×2. Interviewers use them to test whether you can convert a structure into a number, which is the step most candidates handle worst.

Each part, and what it actually does
Contribution margin
Means
What each unit adds after the costs that vary with it.
Driven by
Price − variable cost per unit. As a ratio: (Price − VC) ÷ Price.
So what
The number that decides whether selling more helps at all. Negative contribution means every additional sale loses money, and growth makes things worse — a finding worth saying out loud when you find it.
Breakeven volume
Means
How many units cover the fixed base.
Driven by
Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
So what
Converts an investment question into a demand question you can test: is that volume plausible in this market? That reframing is often the whole analysis.
Breakeven on a price change
Means
How much volume you can afford to lose after raising price, or must gain after cutting it.
Driven by
Required volume change = ΔContribution ÷ new contribution per unit.
So what
The right answer to 'should we cut price 10%'. At 40% contribution, a 10% price cut needs a 33% volume rise just to stand still — a number that usually ends the discussion.
Price elasticity
Means
How much demand moves when price moves.
Driven by
% change in quantity ÷ % change in price. Above 1 is elastic, below 1 inelastic.
So what
Tells you the direction to move price. Inelastic means raising price raises revenue; elastic means the opposite. Almost never given to you — infer it from the client's own history or a competitor's move.
Payback and NPV
Means
Whether an investment clears the bar.
Driven by
Payback = investment ÷ annual cash flow. NPV discounts future flows to today.
So what
Payback is fast and fine for a case; NPV is better when flows are uneven or long-dated. Always ask the client's hurdle rather than inventing one.
Using it without reciting it
  • Say the formula before you use it. It makes your arithmetic auditable and gets you partial credit if you slip.
  • Round aggressively and track the powers of ten. Precision is not the test; magnitude errors are the failure.
  • State what the number means before moving on. A figure with no interpretation scores nothing.
What it does not do. Breakeven assumes the cost split is clean and that fixed costs really are fixed over the range. Elasticity from a single historical move confuses correlation with causation — say so if the data is thin.
Worked example
Applied

Should the client cut price 10% to win share? Current price ₹200, variable cost ₹120, volume 1 M.

Contribution now = ₹80/unit → ₹8 cr total
After a 10% cut: price ₹180, contribution ₹60/unit
Volume needed to hold ₹8 cr = 8 cr ÷ ₹60 = 1.33 M units
Required volume increase = 33%

So the price cut only works if demand is elastic enough to deliver a third more volume — and if capacity can serve it. Framed that way the question answers itself, and you have shown the interviewer exactly what would have to be true.

VRIO and competitive advantageTesting whether a client can actually win — inside market entry, M&A, or a defensibility question.
What it is for

Four tests a resource must pass to produce lasting advantage. It is the counterweight to Porter: Porter asks whether the industry is attractive, VRIO asks whether this client will beat the others in it. Entry cases need both, and candidates usually do only the first.

Each part, and what it actually does
Valuable
Means
Does it let you exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat?
Driven by
Does it raise willingness to pay, or lower cost, in a way customers notice?
So what
If not, it is a capability, not an advantage. Most things a company is proud of fail here.
Rare
Means
Do few competitors have it?
Driven by
How many rivals possess the same resource or could assemble it today?
So what
Valuable but common produces competitive parity — you need it to play, but it wins nothing. Being good at logistics in e-commerce is table stakes.
Inimitable
Means
Is it costly for others to copy or substitute?
Driven by
Historical accident, causal ambiguity, social complexity, patents, accumulated data, network effects, time-compression diseconomies.
So what
The force that determines whether advantage lasts. Anything a competitor can buy is not inimitable, which is why 'we use the best model' is not a moat.
Organised to capture value
Means
Is the firm structured to actually exploit it?
Driven by
Processes, incentives, governance, culture, decision rights.
So what
The most-skipped test and often the real gap. Firms routinely hold a genuine advantage and fail to monetise it because nothing internally is arranged to.
Using it without reciting it
  • Run it on two or three named resources, not on the company in general. 'Our brand' is too vague to test.
  • Use the failing test as the recommendation. If a resource fails inimitability, the advice is about speed; if it fails organisation, the advice is internal.
  • Pair it with Porter in every entry case. Attractive industry plus no advantage equals a market someone else will win.
What it does not do. It is static and inward-looking, and it says nothing about whether the market wants what the resource produces. It also tends to flatter incumbents, since accumulated resources look inimitable right up until a technology shift makes them irrelevant.
Worked example
Applied

An AI startup claiming a data moat. Run VRIO on the data.

Valuable — yes, it improves model output in a way customers can feel. Rare — partly; competitors have similar volumes from public sources. Inimitable — this is where it fails. The data is generated by customer usage that a well-funded rival could replicate in about a year. Organised — yes, retraining pipelines exist.

The moat is a head start, not a moat. That reframing changes the investment recommendation completely, and it is exactly the judgement an interviewer is probing for.

Sequence

How the lenses chain

Strong answers are never one framework. They are a sequence of lenses, each handing off to the next.

01 · Scope
Pin the decision
3CPOR to fix the objective, the metric, the timeline and the constraints. Nothing below is judgeable without this, and the prompt will not give it to you.
02 · Structure
Build the tree
An equation where one exists — profit, revenue, the metric cut. A process or stakeholder cut where it does not. MECE matters more than completeness.
03 · Locate
Segment before diagnosing
Split by geography, product, channel, customer. Find where the movement concentrates. Skipping this step is the most expensive mistake in the sequence.
04 · Diagnose
Open the cheapest branch first
One question that can close a branch beats five that narrow it. Price before volume. Both trends before either branch.
05 · Quantify
Size what you found
Put a number on the gap and on each lever. Unsized findings cannot be prioritised, and unprioritised findings are not advice.
06 · Decide
Compare options honestly
Value, feasibility, risk, time, reversibility. Name the walk-away condition and what would change your mind.
07 · Synthesise
Answer, then why, then risk
Recommendation first, two or three supporting reasons with numbers, key risks, immediate next step. Never a recap of the conversation.

Frameworks that get confused

The mistakes that cost marks are not gaps in knowledge. They are two frameworks that look alike under time pressure.

PairIt is the first whenIt is the second whenThe deciding question
Profitability vs GrowthSomething is broken and you are diagnosing a decline.The business works and the client wants scale.Does the prompt state a decline or a target? Decline is profitability; target is growth.
Revenue branch vs Pricing casePrice is one leaf of a diagnosis you arrived at by elimination.Setting or defending a number is the question itself.Did you reach price by asking, or were you handed it? Handed means pricing case.
